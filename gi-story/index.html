The Revival

Reclaiming our own history.

Honouring where we come from. Deciding where we go next.

In the 1990s, India learned a hard lesson. A US patent was granted for using turmeric to heal wounds, something every Indian grandmother already knew. A European patent claimed a fungicide drawn from the neem tree. A US company patented rice lines bred from basmati.

India challenged each one, and the turmeric and neem patents were struck down. But the message was clear: knowledge without a legal name can be claimed by whoever files the paperwork first.

Together with India's commitments under the WTO's TRIPS agreement, those fights led Parliament to pass the Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration & Protection) Act in 1999. For the first time, Indian law could say that a product belongs to a place, and to the people who make it there.

The Story

From Certificates
to Livelihoods

India's GI story, and why it isn't finished yet

On paper, a GI tag is a finished thing. A producers' body files an application, the Registry examines it, objections are heard, and a certificate is issued. The process is long, but it has a clear end.

Turning that certificate into a living for a village is a different job, and it has no end date. A tag does not raise anyone's price on its own. It needs people who know India's regions first-hand: the soil, the season, the family that has grown one crop for five generations. The same people also have to understand buyers, build a supply chain that holds from the farm gate to the front door, and actually sell.

A GI tag protects a name. Only a fair market protects the people behind it.

The long road from tag to table

Most GIs are registered by a producers' association, a cooperative or a state body. Individual growers must then register as Authorised Users before they can legally sell under the name, and in many regions only a small share ever do. The rest still sell at the nearest mandi, where a GI-tagged harvest is often weighed and priced like any ordinary crop.

That can't be fixed from an office in a big city. It means travelling to the district that owns the tag, staying through a harvest, learning how the produce is grown, dried and stored, and earning the trust of the people who grow it. The work is slow and unglamorous, and it's the only kind that lasts.

That is the road 91GI chose. We source from the region named on each GI, work with the farmer producer organisations, cooperatives and growers who hold it, and pay prices that reflect what the name is actually worth.

A name the world already respects

Other countries learned this long ago. Champagne, Parmigiano Reggiano and Scotch whisky earn a premium because their origin is guarded and their quality holds steady. In 2011, Darjeeling Tea became the first Indian product protected as a GI in the European Union.

India has hundreds more names that deserve the same standing, among them Kashmir saffron, Mithila makhana, Araku Valley coffee and Sangli raisins. They will earn it the same way: by keeping their promise every single time.

GI for every kitchen

A GI tag is not a luxury label. Most of India's GI foods are everyday staples: rice, dal, chillies, jaggery, ghee and spices that Indian families have cooked with for generations. They belong on the dinner table, not just in a festive gift hamper.

Heritage shouldn't be priced like a souvenir. It should be something every family can afford to eat.

So we keep the quality bar high and our margins lean. We work with producers all year, not only at harvest, and we aim for a price that is fair at both ends: to the grower who sold it and the family who buys it.

Every purchase also counts as a vote. When demand for a GI grows, more growers register to use its name, more young people see a future in farming, and the name gets harder to fake.
